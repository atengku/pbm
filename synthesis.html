The Machine Singapore Built

Twenty-one MAS papers on programmable money, read as one document

Azan Tengku · Seeking Alpha Advisors · August 2026

Every claim below is sourced to the primary documents hosted on this site, cited inline as P1–P14 and G1–G7. Click any citation to open the original paper. Nothing here rests on secondary reporting.

The take

Read separately, these are twenty-one technical papers about ledgers. Read in sequence, they are the build log of a single machine: a financial system where policy executes itself at the transaction layer — and a decade-long record of who was given the keys, who was protected, and who was left out of the room.

Three sentences carry the whole corpus. The 2021 economics monograph decided Singapore doesn't need a retail digital dollar but will build the capability anyway P3. The PBM whitepaper then put every rule citizens experience — expiry, merchant locks, denominations — into a wrapper written by whoever creates the scheme, while keeping the money underneath deliberately dumb P1. And the Orchid Blueprint decided who gets to write those wrappers: designated service providers, on a permissioned ledger, to FMI standards P4. Constraint is authored, authorship is licensed, and the licence list is short. Everything else in the corpus is elaboration.

How the machine is built

The money layer was settled in 2021, before most of it was built. The EPG monograph is the constitution: deposits are 92% of the money supply and the banks' cheap, stable funding; a well-designed CBDC is precisely the instrument that could drain them and accelerate a run; therefore no retail issuance — build optionality instead, with one named trigger for reversal: a foreign digital currency gaining traction that prudential rules can't hold back P3. Every design choice downstream complies with that doctrine. PBM rides on private money P1P2. The Blueprint whitelists exactly three monies — regulated stablecoins, tokenised bank liabilities, and a MAS CBDC held in reserve P4. And when the wholesale programme finally touched commercial ground, the money that won was the tokenised bank deposit G5 — the two-tier system digitising itself, exactly as the 2021 paper's protective logic would predict.

The wrapper is the policy surface. The PBM whitepaper is explicit that conditions — validity windows, whitelists, denominations — are parameters set by the PBM Creator, and that the reference design includes a revoke path: expired tokens can be clawed back and the underlying collateral recovered by the creator P1, Fig 15. Two consequences the paper never draws for you. First, every "the system cannot make an exception" a citizen hears is a policy choice executed by code, never a law of mathematics — the revoke function exists in MAS's own spec. Second, breakage on billion-dollar disbursement schemes P2 becomes programmable revenue flowing back to the creator, and no document in this corpus publishes an accounting standard for it.

Decentralisation was tested, proven, and declined. This is the corpus's most under-reported fact. Ubin Phase 2 decentralised the crown jewels of a settlement system — queuing and gridlock resolution — across three platforms without breaking privacy, and concluded in writing that the central operator could be removed, inviting a re-evaluation of MAS's own operator role P6. Six years later the Blueprint reinstated the operator with FMI-grade requirements and found permissionless networks "unlikely to meet the requirements" P4. GL1 then exported that same gatekeeping globally, wearing open-network language P12. Whatever you think of the choice, it was a choice — the corpus contains its own proof that the technology never required it.

The five findings

1. Recourse follows power. Three sightings, one direction. When the counterparties are banks, the state machine grows protective states: Ubin Phase 3 built time-bounded recovery and arbitration into wholesale DvP, with Disputed and Reversed as first-class outcomes P7. Ubin V shipped a public escrow API whose message set includes an explicit dispute action P10. Mandala deployed zero-knowledge proofs so no unencrypted institutional data ever leaves the bank P13. The citizen-facing PBM lifecycle, by contrast, ends at Expired — no dispute state, no appeal path, no protocol-level recourse at all P1. The machinery exists; MAS engineered it, three times, for institutions. Its absence on the retail side is a design decision sitting in plain sight across MAS's own specifications.

2. The consortium is the constant; the public-private membrane is open. The same repertory cast runs from 2017 to 2024 — DBS, OCBC, UOB, JPMorgan, and the clearing incumbent BCS appear from Ubin Phase 1 P5 through the Blueprint P4. The consortium narrowed from eleven banks to MAS-plus-Temasek-plus-JPMorgan by Phase 5 P10; the Partior joint venture (JPM/DBS/Temasek) commercialised that design within a year, then returned as the contractor building BIS Dunbar prototypes P14 — public research to private company to public research's vendor inside twenty-four months. The door has names: Toh Wee Kee authored Ubin Phase 1 as MAS P5 and signs Guardian's framework paper as JPMorgan G7. None of this is alleged impropriety; all of it is structure, and the structure is the finding: the record of what "succeeded" in this programme is substantially written by the parties positioned to monetise the success.

3. The concessions are being drawn before the market exists. The Blueprint creates two chokepoints with no published criteria, tender, or sunset: who operates the Orchid Compatible Ledger, and who is designated to deploy smart contracts — the wrapper-authoring privilege itself P4. GL1 scales the same construct internationally: the shared ledger's governance vacuum, which Dunbar honestly documented (no sovereign will host its liability on a platform it doesn't control P14), is answered by incorporating an Operating Company run by a bank consortium — BNY, Citi, MUFG, JPMorgan's Onyx and SocGen-FORGE are already at the table P12. Whoever holds these seats owns the settlement layer of tokenised finance. The antitrust question — a bank-owned utility setting participation criteria for its competitors — appears nowhere in the corpus.

4. Every revolution ends with the incumbent holding a workstream. SWIFT is accommodated in Ubin V's diplomacy P10, given the ISO 20022 integration path in Mandala P13, and appears as a named bridging use case in the tokenised-funds playbook G6. Custody realism re-centres licensed custodians the rhetoric promised to remove G1. Ant International — the walled-garden threat the 2021 paper worried about P3 — enters the tent as a client of bank-issued tokenised money G5. The pattern is consistent and, frankly, competent: Singapore absorbs challengers by membership. The open question G5 leaves hanging is whether a platform treasury at Ant's scale stays a client forever.

5. The citizen is the missing document. Twenty-one papers, and not one maps whether ordinary people trust programmable money, under what conditions, or with what safeguards they'd accept expiry, merchant locks, and credentialed identity. The adoption-gap paper's "investors at the heart" means institutional investors throughout G4. The economics monograph models banks, flows and runs — not households P3. Privacy engineering of genuine world-class quality exists in this corpus, and it protects bank data P13. The negative is now confirmed across the full set: the citizen-sentiment volume of Singapore's programmable-money programme has not been written.

What it means

The doctrine has converged. "Compliance by design" is the phrase in both the retail PBM whitepaper P1 and the wholesale Mandala report P13: rules encoded ex ante, proofs travelling with the money, funds releasing only when the check passes. From CDC vouchers to interbank capital-flow management, the same idea at two scales. The endgame the corpus describes is a financial system where the distinction between regulation and infrastructure dissolves — policy that executes rather than instructs.

That system will work. The engineering in these papers is serious, the sequencing disciplined, the incumbent management shrewd. What it does not yet have is consent infrastructure. A lifecycle with no dispute state P1, an identity layer with no appeal path G7, concessions with no tender P4P12, breakage with no accounting P1P2 — these are not technical gaps, they are legitimacy gaps, and legitimacy is the one component you cannot retrofit after the first public controversy. The bear case across this entire corpus is not a technology failure; it is an expiring-voucher story, a wrongful blacklisting, a bridge insolvency G6 landing on an architecture nobody was asked to consent to.

Where the edge is

Follow the unassigned seats and the unwritten documents. The designated-deployer licence and OCL operator role P4. The GL1 Operating Company's cap table P12. The tokenised-FX master documentation — whoever drafts it writes the market G5. The finality statute the funds playbook says the next five years turn on G6. The compliance-proof's legal status, jurisdiction by jurisdiction P13. And the one deliverable this whole library proves is missing: the citizen-trust research that would give the machine its licence to operate. The people who authored these papers know their own gaps — the frameworks say so in their own candid moments P14G1G4. The work that fills them is the work worth doing.

Twenty-one papers. One machine. Read the originals — they're all one click away.
