01What this is

MAS and the Association of Banks in Singapore (ABS), delivered by Accenture on Microsoft Azure, prototyped the core functions of a Real-Time Gross Settlement system — fund transfer, queuing, and gridlock resolution (liquidity-saving mechanisms) — decentralised across three platforms (Corda, Hyperledger Fabric, Quorum), each with transaction privacy preserved. The headline finding: it is feasible to remove the central infrastructure operator, and “the role of MAS as an infrastructure operator needs to be re-evaluated.”

02Why it matters

This is the road not taken, on the record. Phase 2 shows decentralised gridlock resolution works and openly invites re-evaluating MAS’s operator role. Six years later the Blueprint reinstates an operator-centric, FMI-grade, permissioned model. That reversal makes P6 the corpus’s counterfactual — proof that Singapore’s permissioned architecture is a chosen preference, not a technical necessity. Keep it to quote back whenever the “the technology requires it” defence appears.

03Key findings

Finding 1 — The road not taken, documented. P2 proves the central operator can be removed and invites re-evaluating MAS’s role. The Blueprint later reinstates it. Decentralisation was tested, found feasible, and declined — a policy choice now on the record.
Finding 2 — Privacy-tech menu established. UTXO / confidential identities (Corda) vs channels (Fabric) vs Constellation+ZKP (Quorum) — the 2017 catalogue that later ZKP work industrialises.
Finding 3 — LSM decentralisation is the genuinely-new result. Gridlock resolution without a single consolidated view was the real cryptographic contribution; everything else was integration.

05The Money Trail

The money story here is quieter and sharper than the tech. The decentralisation finding appears inconvenient to the very parties who co-authored it: removing the central operator removes a fee-and-oversight structure — and the incumbent settlement operator (BCS in the wider lineage) and the banks around the table are the ones whose position that would erode. So the result that “operators can be removed” is documented, then, over the next six years, walked back to an operator-centric model (P4) in which incumbents keep their seats. ABS co-leading is the tell: the banking cartel holds a structural seat at the design table for its own settlement layer, and Accenture, paid to deliver, writes the record. No participant discloses that a decentralised RTGS would have cost them fee income or oversight leverage — the interest that best explains why a feasible design was declined.

06The Gap

What the enthusiasm skips. (1) Finality-in-law: no analysis of whether decentralised gridlock netting satisfies settlement-finality statutes. (2) Operator economics: removing MAS as operator removes a fee/oversight structure nobody models — the silence that later justifies putting the operator back. (3) Centralised-cloud dependency: a “decentralised” ledger running entirely on Azure passes without comment.

08Podcast

Listen · read in your browser

Two hosts, understated. This is the one that quietly embarrasses everything that came after it. In 2017, MAS and the banks build a prototype settlement system with no central operator — the machine runs itself, privately, across three different platforms. And the report actually says out loud: maybe MAS doesn’t need to be the operator anymore. Feasible. Proven. On the record. Then what happens? Six years later the official Blueprint puts the operator firmly back in the middle, permissioned, gated, incumbents keeping their seats. So when someone tells you the closed design was forced by the technology — this document is the receipt that says otherwise. And notice who ran the study: the central bank, yes, but co-led by the banks’ own association, and written up by a consultant paid to deliver it. A decentralised system would have quietly deleted a fee-and-oversight structure those same players sit on. Nobody writes that sentence down. But it’s the most honest explanation for why the road that worked was the road not taken.
