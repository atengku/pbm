01What this is

A MAS Economic Policy Group monograph (Nov 2021) — the economic case assessment that concluded there is no compelling case now for a retail SGD CBDC, while judging it prudent to build the technical and policy capability anyway. It is the policy brain of the whole lineage: the source of Orchid’s “no urgent need” citation and the constraint every later design silently obeys.

02Why it matters

Its analytical spine is bank-funding protection. Deposits are 92% of the money supply and the banks’ cheap, stable funding; an attractive CBDC could drain that base and accelerate runs. Every downstream choice — PBM riding on private money, holding limits, “medium of exchange not store of value” — descends from protecting this. The named reversal trigger is foreign digital-currency substitution, a clock MAS does not control. Its blind spots (household distribution, privacy-as-a-right, sentiment) define the whitespace this library exists to fill.

03Key findings

Finding 1 — Bank funding is the binding constraint. The document’s true centre of gravity: deposits are 92% of money supply. An attractive CBDC threatens that base and speeds runs, so every later design choice is engineered to keep CBDC unattractive as a store of value.
Finding 2 — The real fear is substitution, not innovation. MAS names the scenario that changes its mind — a foreign digital currency prudential regulation “can only defend against up to a point.” The CBDC option is a sovereignty hedge with someone else’s finger on the trigger.
Finding 3 — Distributional and privacy silence. The analysis is entirely systemic — banks, flows, stability. Which households bear the cost as cash fades is unmodelled; privacy appears as a service feature, not a rights baseline. That silence is the fault line public sentiment runs on.

05The Money Trail

This paper names few private actors, which is itself the tell. The beneficiary that never appears as a stakeholder is the banking sector: the entire doctrine — no issuance, private money does the work, holding limits keep any future CBDC a poor store of value — appears engineered to protect the deposit franchise that is 92% of the money supply and the banks’ cheapest funding. The paper discloses this as a “disintermediation risk” in prudential language, but never frames it as an interest being served. Because several of Singapore’s banks are state-linked, deposit-base protection is likely also portfolio protection. The commercial winners are diffuse and off-page — which is precisely why later papers (P4) can hand them concessions without anyone noticing a constituency was chosen back in 2021.

06The Gap

Three silences. (1) No deposit-switching model: the run-acceleration claim is directional; no published elasticity of deposit→CBDC switching for Singapore exists. (2) Distributional incidence: zero analysis of which households bear cost as cash (8% of money) fades before any CBDC arrives. (3) Privacy as afterthought: treated as a regulatable feature, not a rights baseline — the exact issue that decides public consent, left out of the economics.

08Podcast

Listen · read in your browser

Two hosts, calm and clear. Okay, this is the boring-looking 2021 paper that actually decides everything. On the surface it says: Singapore doesn’t need a digital dollar, our payments are already cheap and fast. Fine. But read one layer down and it’s really a document about protecting the banks. Here’s the number that runs the whole thing — bank deposits are ninety-two percent of the money supply, and those deposits are the banks’ cheap funding. A really good central-bank digital currency would let people pull money out of banks in a crisis, fast. So the entire design after this is built to make sure any future digital dollar is deliberately unattractive to hold. Which is clever — but notice who’s being protected, and who isn’t. The paper models banks, capital flows, stability. It does not model you: which households actually lose out as cash quietly disappears, or whether privacy is a right or just a feature they can switch off. Those aren’t technical gaps. They’re the questions nobody with a seat at this table was asked to answer.
