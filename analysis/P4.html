01What this is

MAS’s Nov 2023 infrastructure blueprint (Phase 2 output, with industry-group input). It whitelists three permitted monies — MAS-regulated stablecoins (the Aug 2023 SCS framework), tokenised bank liabilities from licensed banks, and a MAS CBDC — and specifies five building blocks: a Settlement Ledger with atomic settlement and native programmability, a Tokenisation Bridge to account rails, PBM as the programmability protocol, a Name Service, and modular future blocks. It defines the Orchid Compatible Ledger (OCL) and its qualification bar.

02Why it matters

It converts Orchid from experiment to market design. OCL operators face FMI-grade (PFMI) expectations; permissionless networks are ruled “unlikely to meet the requirements”; rulebooks dictate permissible activity — notably that all may transact but only designated service providers may deploy smart contracts. Retail access is indirect only, through licensed FIs after KYC. In other words: the wrapper is the policy surface (P1), and this paper decides who holds the pen — a concession framework published as a technical spec.

03Key findings

Finding 1 — A licensing perimeter drawn in advance. By defining OCL qualification before any market exists, MAS pre-decides the market structure of programmable money. Incumbent settlement operators and licensed banks are grandfathered into the future by specification.
Finding 2 — Permissionless is out, by design. The explicit finding that permissionless networks are “unlikely to meet the requirements” severs retail digital money from public chains — while the Guardian track courts them for wholesale assets. Open for capital-markets optics, closed for the money citizens hold.
Finding 3 — Smart-contract deployment is a licensable privilege. “Deployment of smart contracts may only be done by designated service providers.” Wrapper authorship — the policy surface — becomes a gated concession with no published criteria, tender, or sunset. This is the choke point of the whole architecture.

05The Money Trail

This is where the money stops being abstract. The Blueprint creates two undistributed licences of enormous value and assigns neither in public: who operates the OCL, and who is designated to deploy smart contracts — the wrapper-authoring privilege itself. The natural OCL operator is hiding in the contributor list: BCS, the domestic clearing incumbent, whose fee pool would span national retail disbursement and voucher flows (sized off RedeemSG/CDC volumes in P2). The designated-deployer role likely lands with a short list of licensed providers — and Onyx by J.P. Morgan, a private bank co-authoring the public spec, is exactly the kind of entity positioned to receive it, echoing its role in P1 and the consortium map in P10. None of these contributors discloses a commercial stake in the concession they are helping design. A bank-owned utility setting participation criteria for its own competitors is an antitrust question the paper does not raise.

06The Gap

What the technical framing hides. (1) Designated-deployer capture: whoever wins the role owns the policy-authoring layer for programmable SGD — created with no criteria, no contestability, no sunset. (2) OCL monopoly: the paper gestures at convergence to a single ledger for scale — i.e. a new systemically-important private monopoly at the heart of retail money, with governance unwritten. (3) The Bridge as fragile joint: the Tokenisation Bridge inherits both worlds’ failure modes — the systemic single point the PFMI language circles without naming.

08Podcast

Listen · read in your browser

Two hosts, wry. So if the last few papers were about how the money works, this one’s about who gets the keys. The Blueprint sets up two jobs worth a fortune. One: running the official ledger everything settles on. Two — and this is the sneaky one — being “designated” to deploy the smart contracts. Remember, the smart contract is the wrapper, and the wrapper is where all the rules live. So “who’s allowed to write wrappers” is really “who gets to write the rules of your money.” And here’s the thing: the paper hands out neither job in public. No tender, no criteria, no expiry date. But the likely winners are already in the room — BCS, the clearing incumbent, is the obvious ledger operator; and JPMorgan’s Onyx is helping write the very spec it’s positioned to profit from. None of them disclose that interest. A bank-owned utility writing the rules its competitors have to follow — that’s an antitrust question. The paper just doesn’t ask it. The citizen cost isn’t a fee yet. It’s that a public concession is being drawn before anyone gets to object.
