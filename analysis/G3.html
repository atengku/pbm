01What this is

A MAS technical paper (v1.0, Nov 2023) on the third interoperability approach from the Guardian networks taxonomy: connecting heterogeneous digital-asset networks bilaterally with a common cross-network asset-transfer model. Citing Project Dunbar on the cost of common-platform coordination, it argues bilateral interlinking is the pragmatic alternative, illustrated by case studies in cross-network ABS/trade receivables, discretionary portfolios with alternatives, and cross-network fund distribution. It is explicitly non-policy and non-endorsement.

02Why it matters

The bridge layer is where wholesale interoperability actually happens in the near term — and where risk concentrates. G3 reveals a two-horse strategy: the programme funds interlinking because common platforms are hard, while GL1 (P12) pursues the common platform anyway. It also shows the corpus learning from itself, absorbing Dunbar's sovereign-coordination wall (P14) as the stated reason to interlink rather than unify. But every bridge concentrates custody and finality risk at exactly the point the wider industry has suffered its largest losses, and the paper models mechanics, not failure economics.

03Key findings

Finding 1 — A two-horse strategy, quietly run. G3 argues interlinking because common platforms need coordination sovereigns won't provide; GL1 then pursues the common platform regardless. MAS funds both the pragmatic path and the maximal one — optionality doctrine applied to infrastructure.
Finding 2 — Bridges are the new custody. Every cross-network transfer model concentrates risk at the bridge: locked collateral, mint/burn integrity, cross-chain finality. The industry's worst loss events live exactly there — and the paper models the mechanics, not the failure economics.
Finding 3 — N² economics return, unpriced. Bilateral interlinking recreates the correspondent-mesh cost structure the programme originally set out to escape (P9), now at the token layer, with no accounting of who bears it.

05The Money Trail

The money concentrates at the bridge. Every interlink model locks collateral and mints a representation across networks, so whoever operates the bridge holds the custody and finality control point — the exact spot where the wider industry has taken its largest losses — and the transfer fees. The paper models the mechanics and never allocates the loss: who eats a mint/burn mismatch is unwritten. The unnamed case-study contributors are positioned to run those live corridors. And the headline economic claim — McKinsey's US$4bn-a-year cross-border DLT savings — carries a consultant's undisclosed interest: the firm sizing the prize also sells the transformation mandates to capture it. Bilateral interlinking quietly rebuilds the correspondent-mesh N² costs the programme set out to escape (P9). The corridor logic descends from Jasper-Ubin (P8); the loss-allocation reckoning is deferred to G6; and the common-platform counter-bet is P12.

06The Gap

What the paper leaves out. (1) Bridge-failure loss allocation: no treatment of who bears a mint/burn mismatch or a cross-chain finality break — the single largest risk in wholesale interop is modelled only as mechanics. (2) N² economics: interlinking recreates the correspondent-mesh cost structure at the token layer, unpriced. (3) Bridge-risk quantification: the McKinsey savings figure is published; the offsetting bridge-risk number is not.
