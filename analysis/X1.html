Stage 1 — Intake Analysis

SUBJECT: "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System" — nine pages, posted to the metzdowd cryptography mailing list on 31 Oct 2008, six weeks after Lehman Brothers failed (15 Sep 2008). The starting point of the entire timeline this library documents. SOURCE / RELIABILITY: Primary text, universally available and unaltered — but the only document in this corpus whose author is unverified. "Satoshi Nakamoto" is a pseudonym; the person or group behind it has never been established. Treat the text as Tier 1, the authorship as Tier 3. KEY CLAIMS (from the paper itself): a purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash, allowing online payments to move without any financial institution; double-spending solved by proof-of-work over a public chain rather than by a trusted third party; the network needs "minimal structure"; privacy by pseudonymous keys rather than identity. Implemented weeks later with a fixed 21m issuance cap (in code, not in the paper) and a genesis block (3 Jan 2009) embedding that day's Times of London headline: "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks." SIGNIFICANCE: every document that follows on this timeline — BIS 2018 [X2], Lagarde 2018 [X3], BIS 2020 [X4], and the whole MAS build from Ubin to the PBM whitepaper [P5]→[P1] — is downstream of the possibility this paper proved: money can move at scale without banks. ROUTE: → timeline anchor, Oct 2008. The counter-idea the rest of the corpus answers.

Stage 3 — Research Note: The Authorship Question

The hypothesis on the table

The question asked of this file: could the paper have come from the same actors — the central-banking establishment — as a controlled way of testing digital currencies and their effect on a population?

What feeds the suspicion

The timing is surgical — six weeks after Lehman, landing exactly when trust in banks was at its generational low. The cryptography is establishment-grade: Bitcoin runs on SHA-256, a hash standard designed and published by the NSA, and the concept of state-adjacent interest in anonymous digital cash is on the record as early as the NSA's own 1996 paper "How to Make a Mint: The Cryptography of Anonymous Electronic Cash." The author's operational discipline — flawless pseudonymity for years, a clean exit in 2011, an untouched fortune of roughly a million coins — reads more like an institution's tradecraft than a hobbyist's. And the outcome pattern fits a probe: within a decade the official sector had its taxonomy [X2], its permission speech [X3], its bloc doctrine [X4], and its pilots (Ubin, 2016 [P5]) — a remarkably fast harvest of a supposedly hostile experiment.

What cuts against it

The idea has a documented 25-year public lineage through the cypherpunk community — Chaum's ecash (1982), Back's hashcash (1997), Dai's b-money (1998), Szabo's bit gold — evolving in an openly anti-state milieu, not a lab. The genesis message is a taunt at the bailout system, an odd signature for its supposed author. The early code was idiosyncratic in ways institutional software is not. The BIS's own contemporaneous posture was hostile ("Bitcoin not the answer to a cashless society," Mar 2018) — consistent with an institution reacting, not directing. And the incentive logic fails: an establishment wanting to test digital money does not need to release an uncontrollable, permissionless global network — it builds sandboxes, which is precisely what MAS then did [P5]→[P10].

Verdict — and the read that survives either way

Under this file's confidence tiering the authorship hypothesis is Tier 3: state it as a question, never as a fact. The chronology is consistent with reaction, not origination. But the functional claim is Tier 1 and it is the one that matters: whoever wrote the paper, Bitcoin became the world's live, unpaid experiment in digital money — seventeen years of free field data on adoption, custody behaviour, volatility tolerance, loss, theft, crime and sentiment, at population scale — and the official corpus visibly harvested the technology while inverting its properties. You do not need to have authored an experiment to own its results.

The population-effects contrast is the sharpest line: what Bitcoin tested, it tested on volunteers — people who chose self-custody, irreversibility and bearer risk. What the voucher stack deploys — expiry, merchant locks, revocability — is tested on enrollees, a national population that never opted in and is not shown the clock P1. The timeline on the brief reads it correctly: chronology, not proof of a single hand — and the clustering is the point worth interrogating.
