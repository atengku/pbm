01What this is

MAS’s first retail digital-SGD report (FTIG, Nov 2022, foreword by Chief FinTech Officer Sopnendu Mohanty). It introduces Purpose Bound Money publicly, frames the “capability without commitment” doctrine, and documents four live industry trials run on tokenised bank deposits and one regulated SGD stablecoin — never on a MAS-issued CBDC. PBM is presented as a third model beyond programmable payment and programmable money.

02Why it matters

This is the why behind PBM. Beneath the monetary-futures language, the motivating problems are administrative: voucher-scheme fragmentation (RedeemSG vs Grab silos), misuse/deviation risk, admin overhead, and merchant settlement lag of days-to-weeks on billion-dollar disbursement schemes. It is also the on-record statement of the doctrine every later document assumes — that retail CBDC is a defensive option held in reserve, its named trigger being foreign digital currency gaining local traction.

03Key findings

Finding 1 — Optionality is the strategy, not indecision. “No urgent need, build anyway” is deliberate: accumulate issuance capability while deferring the political decision indefinitely. The reversal trigger MAS names is currency substitution — a clock set by USD-stablecoin adoption curves, not by MAS.
Finding 2 — PBM was born as voucher plumbing. The genuine problems are the S$1.5bn Household Support Package, CDC vouchers, and merchant float. The monetary-futures framing is the wrapper; RedeemSG’s pain points are the collateral.
Finding 3 — Private money does the work. Every trial ran on tokenised deposits or a stablecoin. The state supplies the standard; the banks supply the money — the two-tier system absorbing the innovation rather than being disrupted by it.

05The Money Trail

The report presents its trial partners as neutral collaborators. Read as a money map, most are positioned to monetise the very machinery the paper standardises: DBS, OCBC and UOB settle the tokenised deposits PBM rides on; Grab, Fazz and NETS supply the wallets and acceptance; OGP operates the live disbursement rail. The sharpest, unspoken number is merchant float: current voucher claims settle after days-to-weeks, and that working-capital lag — on billion-dollar CDC-scale flows — currently earns someone (scheme administrators and their banks) a return the paper never names. PBM’s instant unwrap appears to destroy that float without disclosing who loses it. Temasek and JPMorgan’s later JV (Partior, per P10) commercialised the deposit-token rails this trial de-risked. The non-disclosure is structural: commercial interest is disclosed only for the regulators and multilaterals, not for the trial participants best placed to profit.

06The Gap

Three things the paper never says. (1) Who earns the float: days-to-weeks settlement lag on CDC/RedeemSG claims is working-capital extraction from heartland merchants; nobody in the paper asks who currently books it. (2) Holder recourse on expiry: no trial is described as testing what a citizen can do when conditional money lapses. (3) Trial-to-policy laundering: “trials” with named commercial partners generate the evidence base that later justifies the standard those same partners implement — a closed evidentiary loop.
