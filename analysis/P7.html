01What this is

MAS and SGX (co-chaired by Sopnendu Mohanty and Tinku Gupta; tech partners Anquan, Deloitte, Nasdaq) settled tokenised Singapore Government Securities against central-bank cash-depository receipts across separate ledgers, trade-by-trade, with settlement finality. Delivery-versus-Payment is encoded in smart contracts, and the design wraps it in institutional-grade protections: multi-signature account controls, contract locks with secret disclosure, time-bounded asset recovery, and arbitration for disputes.

02Why it matters

Its most valuable content today is comparative. The wholesale state machine includes Disputed and Reversed states and an arbitration path; the retail PBM lifecycle (P1) ends at “Expired” with no appeal. Recourse was engineered for banks and omitted for citizens — a design fingerprint showing protection follows institutional power. P3 also begins the re-centralisation: one phase after P6 showed operators removable, it deliberately re-centres a Recognised Market Operator.

03Key findings

Finding 1 — Recourse was built where the counterparties are banks. Time-bounded recovery, arbitration on dispute, and Reversed/Disputed states are exactly the contestability machinery absent from the citizen-facing PBM lifecycle. Protection follows power.
Finding 2 — The decentralisation retreat begins here. One phase after P2 proved operator-removal feasible, P3 re-centres a Recognised Market Operator. The permissioned trajectory that ends in the Blueprint starts in 2018.
Finding 3 — “Distributed, not decentralised,” said out loud. The report’s own sidebar concedes every layer is a centric implementation — the honest sentence the rest of the corpus avoids.

05The Money Trail

The comparative reading is the finding, and it has a money dimension. The people who get engineered recourse here are banks and market participants; the party co-chairing is SGX, the incumbent central securities depository and Recognised Market Operator — which has a direct commercial stake in whichever on-ledger settlement future wins, and whose Marketnode venture (an SGX/Temasek JV) later inherits these designs. So the entity that co-authors the future of securities settlement is also the incumbent it would disrupt — classic incumbent absorption — and the report keeps the RMO central, protecting that position. Meanwhile the arbitration and reversal machinery MAS demonstrably owns is never mapped onto the retail PBM lifecycle where citizens would need it. The asymmetry is the story: where the counterparties have commercial power, the design grows an appeal path; where they are citizens, it does not. SGX’s stake is only partially disclosed; the recourse asymmetry is disclosed nowhere, because no one in the corpus cross-reads these two documents.

06The Gap

What the wholesale framing leaves for citizens to notice. (1) The recourse asymmetry vs P1: banks get Disputed/Reversed states and arbitration; the retail PBM holder gets “Expired” and off-chain discretion. (2) On-chain arbitration as an unused template: MAS already owns a citizen-usable dispute mechanism and has not reused it. (3) Custody-revenue impact: T+2→T+0 compression’s effect on SGX custody margins is unexamined by the co-chair who earns them.

08Podcast

Listen · read in your browser

Two hosts, pointed but calm. Here’s the detail that should bother you. In 2018, MAS and the stock exchange build a settlement system for banks trading government bonds. And look at what they carefully build in: multi-signature controls, time limits, a way to recover assets, and — this is the key word — arbitration. If a trade goes wrong, there’s a dispute state, a reversal state, a path to appeal. Now hold that next to the system built for ordinary people, the purpose-bound voucher. That one has one ending: expired. No dispute, no appeal, no reversal. The exact machinery the banks got, the citizens didn’t. And MAS already owns it — they designed it here — they just never reused it for the public. Why? Follow the table. The bank side is co-chaired by the exchange that profits from the settlement business, and its recourse protects institutional money. The citizen side had no one at the table whose revenue depended on giving people a way to complain. Same architecture, opposite recourse. That’s not a technical limitation. That’s a decision about who is worth protecting.
