01What this is

A BIS Innovation Hub (Singapore Centre) project with the RBA, Bank of Korea, Bank Negara Malaysia and MAS, with Bank of France contributions: compliance-by-design. Jurisdictional rules — sanctions, capital-flow management — are encoded into a peer-to-peer protocol that generates a cryptographic proof of compliance, attached to a wholesale-CBDC or SWIFT instruction, gating fund release. Three components: a P2P messaging layer, a rules engine and a proof engine, using zero-knowledge proofs and multi-party computation.

02Why it matters

Mandala is the direct fix for the compliance-cost root cause that the 2019 trilateral report P9 identified — and it uses the same "compliance-by-design" phrase the retail PBM whitepaper P1 uses, retail and wholesale converging on one doctrine. It is also the strongest privacy engineering in the corpus. The catch is who it protects: the regulatory state renders itself as protocol, and the protection points one way.

03Key findings

Finding 1 — Compliance becomes infrastructure. Once rules are encoded and proofs travel with money, the line between "regulation" and "payment system" dissolves. Mandala is the wholesale twin of the PBM wrapper P1 — both make policy execute at the transaction layer, now spanning citizen vouchers to interbank capital-flow management.
Finding 2 — Privacy tech, deployed where the protected party is a bank. ZKP and MPC ensure no unencrypted data ever leaves the institution — world-class privacy engineering, applied to institutional data. The recourse-and-privacy machinery keeps appearing on the wholesale side first P7P10.
Finding 3 — Asymmetric transparency — the one-way mirror. The same design gives regulators real-time monitoring of institutions while citizens’ visibility into the rules applied to them gains nothing. Privacy for the bank; surveillance capability for the state; the direction of the mirror is never noted.

05The Money Trail

Mandala’s beneficiaries split cleanly. The privacy engineering — zero-knowledge proofs and MPC so that no unencrypted data ever leaves a bank — protects institutional data; the real-time monitoring it enables flows to regulators. The one party the architecture gives nothing is the citizen, who receives no visibility into the rules applied to them: a one-way mirror whose direction the report never notes. Commercially, the proofs are tested against Partior and the R3-built Dunbar prototypes P14 — the private and public strands re-braiding, each validating its own rails as "compliant" — and SWIFT is re-accommodated through the ISO 20022 CR1357 pathway, the incumbent handed the integration route. None of these downstream positions is disclosed as commercial interest. It answers the de-risking problem of P9, but the surplus of visibility it creates accrues upward.

06The Gap

Three gaps. (1) Legal status of the proof: whether a cryptographic compliance proof is recognised, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, is the make-or-break question and is unaddressed. (2) Asymmetric transparency: regulators gain real-time institutional monitoring; citizens’ visibility into the rules gains nothing, and the direction of the mirror goes unremarked. (3) Rule ossification: encoded CFM and sanctions rules must update at the speed of regulation; the protocol-governance question from Jasper–Ubin P8 returns wearing a compliance badge, unanswered — while the cost-per-compliant-payment stays unquantified.
